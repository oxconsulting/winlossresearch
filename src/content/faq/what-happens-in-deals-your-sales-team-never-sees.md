# What happens in deals that your sales team never sees?

Most B2B deals include a parallel track of activity a vendor's sales team never sees: private conversations between stakeholders, objections raised and resolved without the vendor present, and a final internal recommendation shaped by people who never joined a call. What the sales team experiences is a partial account of a larger process.

That parallel track runs through the [buying committee](/glossary/buying-committee/), the group of stakeholders who collectively decide whether to purchase. A rep's view of a deal is built from scheduled calls, sent materials, and whatever a champion chooses to relay afterward. The committee's actual deliberation, an [internal buying discussion](/glossary/internal-buying-discussion/) among people who work together and have no reason to manage what they say to each other, happens somewhere the sales team was never invited.

Three things routinely happen in that unseen track. First, stakeholders the vendor never met weigh in, sometimes decisively, on criteria the sales conversation never addressed: budget mechanics, prior experience with the vendor, or a competing internal priority for the same funding. Second, the champion's own understanding of the committee's reasoning is often incomplete, which means even a well-intentioned debrief after a loss reflects a partial and secondhand account rather than what was actually said. Third, the timing of the real decision frequently has nothing to do with the vendor's forecasted next step. A committee's private consensus can form days before the vendor's next scheduled call, which means the deal was effectively decided while the sales team was still treating it as open.

None of this reflects a flaw in how the rep ran the deal. It reflects the structure of how group purchase decisions get made: through conversations among people who work together, not through the vendor's sales process. A vendor cannot observe a conversation it was never going to be part of, regardless of how strong the visible relationship is.

The gap tends to widen with deal size and committee complexity. A small, single-buyer purchase has less unseen territory simply because fewer people are involved in reaching the decision. A larger enterprise evaluation, with security review, legal sign-off, and a competing internal initiative for the same budget, has considerably more surface area for something to happen that never reaches the vendor's side. This is one reason larger deals are harder to forecast accurately: the ratio of visible activity to actual decision-making activity gets worse as the committee grows.

What a vendor can do is treat this gap as a research question rather than an unavoidable blind spot. Independent interviews conducted after a decision, when a buyer has no ongoing relationship left to manage, recover a meaningfully more complete account of what happened in that unseen track: who was actually in the room, what they raised, and how the recommendation the champion built either survived or fell apart once it reached people the vendor never spoke with. That account is also more useful in aggregate than on any single deal. A pattern that repeats across several closed opportunities, the same kind of stakeholder objection, the same kind of late-forming consensus, is a finding a GTM team can act on before the next evaluation starts, rather than a story specific to one account that offers no guidance for the next one.
