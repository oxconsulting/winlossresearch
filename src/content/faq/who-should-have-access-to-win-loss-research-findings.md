# Who should have access to win/loss research findings?

Marketing, product, sales, and customer success should all have access to win/loss research findings, not just the function that commissioned the research. Each team hears a different signal in the same interviews: marketing hears messaging and perception, product hears roadmap and evaluation friction, sales hears competitive positioning, and CS hears unmet post-sale expectations.

## Why Sales-Only Distribution Is the Default Failure

Win/loss programs are often positioned as sales enablement from the start, and scoped, analyzed, and delivered accordingly. The output, competitive intel, objection handling, rep coaching, has real value, and it also represents a fraction of what a full set of independent buyer interviews contains. Routing findings exclusively through the sales org is how the rest of that value gets left behind, not because anyone decides deliberately to withhold it, but because sales-only distribution is the default path once a program is framed as a sales tool.

## What Each Function Needs From the Same Interviews

Marketing needs to hear how buyers felt about the company and product before the evaluation started, what shifted their perception during it, and what they were left thinking once the decision was made, because that's where messaging either builds momentum or loses it. Product needs to hear about the evaluation step where confidence in the roadmap broke down, or the moment an integration story stopped holding up under scrutiny. CS needs to hear what post-sale expectations were set during the evaluation that nobody corrected. Each function is asking a different question of the same research base, and the signal changes depending on who's asking.

## Someone Has to Own Making It Happen

Access doesn't distribute itself once a report exists. It requires someone with the mandate and the cross-functional visibility to route findings deliberately, usually the CMO or whoever commissioned the research, actively making sure each function gets the material relevant to it rather than a single summary slide sent to everyone. Without an active owner, findings tend to stop wherever they entered the organization.

## Access Doesn't Mean Everyone Sees Everything

Broad access doesn't mean every function needs the full raw interview transcripts or every finding in undifferentiated form. Product doesn't need the pricing objection detail relevant only to sales, and sales doesn't need the granular roadmap feedback relevant only to product. The goal is that every function that needs a signal has access to it, not that every function wades through material irrelevant to what they own. Structuring distribution this way, by relevance rather than blanket access, is what keeps each function actually engaging with the findings instead of skimming past a document that's mostly not for them.

## What Restricted Access Costs a Program

When access is restricted by default rather than by design, the cost compounds quietly. A positioning gap surfaced in an interview reaches product marketing eventually, if at all, often after a competitor has already capitalized on it. A roadmap concern sits inside a sales-only report until a customer churns for the same reason a buyer described months earlier. The research was conducted once, at real cost in buyer time and program budget, and its value gets realized only in the fraction of the organization that happened to see it. Wide, deliberate access is what lets a single research investment pay off across every function it touches, instead of just the one that requested it.
