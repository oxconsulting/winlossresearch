# Message recall

Message recall is what a buyer can actually reconstruct about a vendor's positioning after the decision has been made, weeks or months removed from the pitch. It is nearly always a smaller, blurrier, and more selective version of what the vendor believes it delivered. Buyers do not carry a transcript of a demo into their next meeting. They carry an impression, and message recall is a measurement of what that impression actually contains.

Recall matters more than delivery because delivery is not the thing that shapes a decision. What shapes the decision is whatever the buyer still has in their head when they talk to the next stakeholder, write the internal justification, or compare vendors from memory rather than from notes. A feature explained perfectly in a demo that the buyer cannot recall three weeks later did not function as a decision input. It functioned as a moment that happened and then evaporated.

Recall tends to fail in a specific pattern: buyers lose the precise language and the product name faster than they lose the underlying impression. One buyer, describing a competitor after a decision, could not name the product and misdescribed a specific feature, but retained a clear conclusion: the platform felt easy to use because of how its training materials were organized. The feature name was gone. The conclusion was intact, and the conclusion was the thing that actually drove behavior. This is the normal shape of message recall in a multi-vendor evaluation, not an exception to it.

Because recall degrades this way, asking a buyer directly whether a message "resonated" produces unreliable answers. Buyers are not holding a scorecard of positioning decisions, and asking them to evaluate specific language after the fact invites a polite, retrofitted answer rather than genuine recall. A more reliable approach is to ask what they remember unprompted, in their own words, and treat whatever survives that test as the real signal. What a buyer volunteers without prompting is a far better measure of message recall than what they confirm when asked leading questions.

Low recall does not always mean the message was poorly written. It sometimes means the message was accurate but never salient enough to survive a multi-vendor, multi-week evaluation process. Distinguishing a wording problem from a salience problem is one of the most useful outputs a win/loss interview can produce for a product marketing team.
