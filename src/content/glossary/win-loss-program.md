# Win/Loss Program

A win/loss program is the ongoing, repeatable practice of interviewing buyers after closed deals to identify patterns in why business is won and lost. The distinction that matters is cadence: a program runs on a defined, recurring schedule, rather than existing as a single research engagement commissioned once and never repeated.

That distinction shapes almost everything else about how a program is scoped, staffed, and evaluated. A one-time engagement answers a specific question and concludes. A program keeps answering that question as market conditions, competitors, and buyer expectations shift underneath it.

## Program vs. Engagement

Teams often start with a single engagement: a defined set of interviews, typically 20 to 30, run against a specific question a leader wants answered. Why did the team lose three consecutive Enterprise deals? Why did win rate drop in a specific segment? A single engagement produces findings, a synthesis, and a readout, and then it's done.

A program takes that same methodology and repeats it on a cadence, often quarterly or semi-annually. The reason a one-time engagement isn't sufficient on its own is that findings decay. Buyer expectations shift, competitors ship new capabilities, and a message that resonated eighteen months ago may no longer land the way it did when the research was run. A program treats win/loss research as an ongoing input into GTM strategy rather than a project with a defined end date.

This is also where the internal-versus-external decision matters most. A program run entirely by an internal team faces a structural conflict of interest that a single engagement can sometimes work around by bringing in outside help for one cycle. Sustaining that neutrality across an ongoing program is harder to do internally, since the same team that owns the relationship with sales and product will eventually own the interviews too.

## What a Program Typically Includes

A defined win/loss program specifies several elements up front, rather than deciding them ad hoc each cycle: an interview cadence (how often interviews run), a target ratio of loss to win interviews, a defined research window relative to deal close, a consistent questionnaire that gets refined but not reinvented each cycle, and a distribution mechanism for getting findings to the functions that need to act on them.

Programs that skip this scoping tend to drift into the activity-metrics trap: treating "we ran five interviews this quarter" as success, regardless of whether any interview changed a decision. A program's real measure of success is whether findings moved something, not whether the cadence was maintained.

## Common Misunderstanding

Teams sometimes use "win/loss program" and "win/loss research" interchangeably, but the terms describe different things. Win/loss research is the methodology, the practice of interviewing buyers to understand deal outcomes. A win/loss program is the organizational commitment to running that methodology on an ongoing basis, with defined ownership, budget, and cadence. A company can conduct win/loss research once without ever building a program around it.

Consider a team that commissions a single round of interviews after a bad quarter, gets useful findings, and then never repeats the exercise. That team did win/loss research. It did not build a win/loss program, and the findings from that single round will be the only data point the team has eighteen months later, even as the market has moved on.
